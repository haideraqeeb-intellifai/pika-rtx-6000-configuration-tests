Which chunk length and model-copy layout helps?

Measurements on one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell, 95.59 GiB. Same 49.408-second podcast, two steps, 24 fps. Every chunk uses the original image as first and last conditioning and can generate independently. No claim of seamless joins.

Transformer = the model that generates the video representation. VAE = the decoder that turns it into visible frames. MPS = NVIDIA’s service that lets separate GPU processes share execution capacity.

Result at a glance

Fastest mean in this follow-up: 20 chunks · 1 transformer / 2 VAE · MPS, 223.6 seconds across 3 run(s), versus 237.3 seconds for the matched 20-chunk, one-transformer/one-VAE control (n=2). Measured throughput difference: +6.1%. Peak VRAM: 41.3 GiB.

My recommendation

Use 20 chunks · 1 transformer / 2 VAE · MPS for the best measured speed/memory tradeoff in this workload: 223.6 seconds including checks, 219.6 seconds until the assembled, fully decoded video is ready, and 41.3 GiB peak memory.

The selection rule is practical: among configurations with at least two clean runs and within 2% of the fastest observed mean, choose the smallest memory footprint. This is a tradeoff rule, not a statistical proof that their speeds are equal. The smallest mean was 223.6 seconds with 20 chunks · 1 transformer / 2 VAE · MPS.

Why this recommendation, quality limits, and the command to run it

Did extra model copies help?

Extra VAE decoder copies helped under MPS. Holding MPS and the chunk plan fixed, one transformer with one VAE averaged 239.8 seconds; one transformer with two VAEs averaged 223.6 seconds, a 7.3% throughput improvement. This is the direct decoder-copy comparison.

Compared with ordinary scheduling, one transformer with two VAEs under MPS took 223.6 seconds, versus 237.3 seconds for the ordinary one-transformer/one-VAE pipeline: 6.1% more throughput for the same video and chunk plan. Compared with the improved background-export one-copy option, the difference is 4.2%.

Four VAEs took 224.8 seconds and 53.7 GiB, while two transformers plus two VAEs took 225.6 seconds and 68.1 GiB. The table below contains the remaining allocations. More copies do not add compute units; they change how stages compete for the same GPU.

This updates the earlier full-model-copy result. Here, text encoding happens on the GPU once and is released before independent stage workers load. The previous CPU-text-encoding penalty is absent. With one transformer, denoising stays sequential, while multiple VAEs decode completed chunks concurrently. Two-transformer tests also overlap denoising calls, but extra transformer copies have not produced a large full-video gain.

The speed/quality tradeoff

All these runs produce the same 49.408-second podcast audio and 1,186 video frames at 24 fps. Shorter chunks create more joins: 20 chunks means 19 joins. Every 20-chunk join was flagged by the simple luma-change screen. The reference portrait remains recognizable in the inspected contact sheet, but this does not establish smooth motion or lip sync. Watch the output before accepting this chunking policy.

Ordinary loading and MPS variants with the same chunk plan produce identical output hashes in the completed checks. The alternative resident loading backend changes pixels; its technical similarity is reported separately below. Neither a small pixel difference nor matching frame counts establishes equal perceptual quality.

How confident is this?

These are measurements from one GPU, one prompt, one portrait and one audio recording. Most initial screens have one sample; promising settings have repeats. Run order was adaptive, not randomized. Gaps below a few seconds should be treated cautiously. The two earlier timings affected by competing jobs are excluded, and the user-cancelled trial has no timing. The resumed ownership audit is bundled.

The measured lifecycle starts fresh preparation and worker processes for each video. It includes model loading, encoding and validation. Source-audio splitting and calibration are excluded. Sustained warm-service throughput, production concurrency and multi-GPU speed are not measured here.

What runs on the GPU?

1. Prepare text, image and audio conditioning on the GPU once per video, reusing identical text/image inputs. Release the encoder. 2. Keep the selected transformer and VAE worker processes alive across chunks. 3. Each transformer takes an available independent chunk; each VAE takes a finished latent. 4. Restore chunk order and mux the original audio.

The original image conditions both ends of each chunk. It does not force the first and last output pixels to equal the portrait, and it does not prove smooth joins.

How to read these numbers

  • Fresh-process timings include GPU conditioning preparation, encoder release, stage-worker startup/load, generation, latent handoff, encoding, full media validation and assembly. Initial source-audio splitting and memory calibration are excluded. The total column additionally includes audio checks, join screening and contact-sheet generation. These are not warm-service latency measurements. OS file caches and persistent kernel/compiler caches may remain between runs; any initialization or compilation inside the recorded interval is counted.
  • Throughput is 3600 divided by the measured complete-video time. It describes repeated execution with this lifecycle; sustained multi-job throughput was not measured in this follow-up.
  • GPU sampling is about every 0.2 s during preparation and stage workers. Peak is sampled whole-device memory, not PyTorch allocated memory. GPU utilization means activity during sampling; it is not a measurement of SM occupancy or percent of peak compute throughput. Per-node allocated-memory data are in raw results. Final assembly/QA is outside that GPU sampling interval.
  • Each stage runs in a separate process. Overlapping worker calls are recorded; simultaneous GPU kernel execution is unverified. Ordinary CUDA contexts time-share compute according to NVIDIA’s architecture documentation. A model copy adds no GPU compute units.
  • Per-stage/node timings overlap and must not be added to infer elapsed time. Loader-node timing omits deferred weight transfers that occur inside inference. Full node timings and measured model-loaded/offloaded memory are downloadable.
  • Chunk lengths are aligned to H3's 17k+5 inference grid; delivered frames total 1186 in every run (49.4167 s of video versus 49.408 s of source audio, an 8.7 ms frame-grid difference). Changing chunk count changes audio boundaries, seeds applied to each region, and anchor frequency. Compare scheduling quality within each chunk plan, then judge changed chunk lengths visually.
  • Original source WAV was unavailable; this source audio was decoded from the reference report MP4. Native model canvas 736×1280, crop720×1280, export1080×1920. Seeds9901+chunk index. No resolution or step reduction.
  • The 65/35 MPS attempt in the previous report failed with GPU lost. GPU recovery was confirmed before this follow-up. The main matrix uses ordinary scheduling. Follow-up checks use standard 100/100 MPS on the best two-transformer layout and a matched one-transformer/one-VAE control, with attached clients verified; the failed 65/35 configuration is excluded.

Memory guards, excluded timings and failed attempts

Two maximum-length chunks calibrate each stage. For 20 chunks, three transformers plus one VAE would have a conservative summed envelope of 94.0 GiB and four plus one VAE 122.7 GiB, above the 86.0 GiB admission budget. These larger transformer counts were not executed; fitting weights alone does not establish enough activation memory. Initial layouts use a 90% memory admission ceiling; the capacity sweep uses 95%, leaving at least 4.8 GiB estimated headroom. Three transformers plus one VAE uses the measured isolated three-transformer pool peak plus the isolated VAE peak; other layouts sum isolated per-worker peaks. These are admission estimates; actual sampled memory is reported per run. Timings affected by another GPU workload are retained as excluded attempts and never enter the averages. A memory-rejected layout has no measured latency. This is a conservative admission decision, not a measured out-of-memory failure or proof that the layout could never fit.

A warm eight-GPU service compared with a fresh-process one-GPU run changes both the lifecycle and GPU count. Validate against an equally warmed one-GPU baseline before calling a result superlinear hardware scaling.

For the best allocation in this model, matching eight independent workers requires about 17% shorter stage service times with zero added scheduling overhead, or 25% shorter with 10% overhead. Those dedicated-stage gains have not been measured. This calculation assumes all stage times scale by the same factor and transfers remain below the bottleneck.

Validation hardware tests: actual eight-card PCIe/P2P topology, all-pairs and simultaneous bandwidth/latency for these tensor sizes, host NUMA/RAM bandwidth, isolated warm stage service distributions and activation peaks, CPU/export/storage capacity under eight jobs, then sustained eight-worker and stage-split end-to-end runs with frame/audio checks and latency percentiles.

These are one-GPU measurements. Independent workers on eight identical GPUs have an ideal throughput ceiling of eight times the comparable one-GPU worker, before contention in CPU, storage and transfers.
