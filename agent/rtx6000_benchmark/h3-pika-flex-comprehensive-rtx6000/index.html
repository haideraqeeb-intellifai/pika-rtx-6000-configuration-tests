What the components do

  • Text/image/audio preparation builds the conditioning. In the newer workers, the GPU prepares it once, reuses identical text/image inputs, and releases the encoder before generation.
  • Transformer (T) denoises a compact video representation. Multiple copies can work on independent chunks, while sharing the same physical GPU.
  • VAE converts that representation into visible frames.
  • Encoding turns frames into the final video file and joins the original audio.
  • MPS lets separate GPU processes share execution capacity. Loaded copies do not add compute units or memory bandwidth.
Chained: denoise chunk 1 → decode its last delivered frame → start chunk 2 → …
Independent pipeline: transformer workers → queue of completed chunks → VAE workers → encode and assemble
Staged test: finish all denoising → release transformers → load VAEs and decode

“3 transformers then 2 VAEs” and “3 transformers + 1 VAE together” are different schedules. Both were tested; the tables explicitly distinguish them. Overlapping timed calls are verified where reported; simultaneous GPU kernel execution was not established by the profiler.

Memory and output tradeoff

Peak VRAM increased from 41.3 to 43.9 GiB. The default controls match the historical winning video bytes. The two fast outputs match each other exactly, but differ from default: downsampled 180 × 320 SSIM is 0.980473. Contact sheets show similar subject appearance and framing; this is not a perceptual-equivalence or lip-sync certification. All four outputs have 1,186 frames at 24 fps and passed full video/audio decoding. Audio lag and duration difference were 0 ms at 8 kHz. All 19 joins still trigger the transition screen in both modes.

Recommendation: use 1T/2V MPS with fast mode for this independent-chunk workload, subject to your visual review. Fast mode was not tested on the strict previous-last-frame chained configuration.

Serialized preparation plus latent payload is 0.975 GiB per video. Assumptions span 1–25 GiB/s aggregate effective transfer bandwidth, 0.1–5 ms per logical handoff, 40 handoffs and one direct or two host-mediated traversals. Full decoded frames are encoded locally on VAE GPUs. CPU, storage and model-startup contention can reduce real throughput.

Per-GPU memory must include weights, activations and queued tensors. The historical default-mode independent worker peaks at 41.3 GiB; isolated transformer/VAE calibration peaks are 28.8/7.7 GiB. These figures do not validate eight-worker host RAM, NUMA, I/O or peak memory under load.

Is greater-than-8× plausible? It is a hypothesis under favorable stage-efficiency assumptions, not a result. Under the historical default-mode assumptions, the central stage-split model trails eight independent workers. Relative to that historical default-mode baseline, the 1/4/3 split would need about a 17% uniform stage-time reduction with zero added overhead, or 25% with 10% overhead, to match the ideal independent-worker rate. Comparing a warm eight-GPU service with a fresh-process one-GPU run would confound lifecycle and hardware scaling.

What must be measured on the actual eight-GPU machine

  • GPU topology, peer access, all-pairs and simultaneous transfer bandwidth/latency using these tensor sizes; direct versus host-mediated paths.
  • Warm isolated stage times, queue depth, active/reserved VRAM and model residency on each device.
  • Eight-worker CPU encoding, RAM bandwidth, NUMA effects, storage and assembly throughput.
  • Sustained independent-worker and stage-pipeline runs, matched warm one-GPU baseline, p50/p95 video latency and steady-state throughput.
  • Identical frame/audio checks and human review of joins on the final output.
